# Evidence reference

Evidence exists to make the next teaching decision more accurate, not to create paperwork.

## Evidence ladder

A rough strength ordering:

    recognition
    < recall
    < explanation
    < representative application
    < transfer

This is not a universal scoring formula.

## Dimensions that matter

Consider:

- independence: how much help was needed?
- cueing: free generation or recognition?
- delay: immediate repetition or later retrieval?
- novelty: same example or changed context?
- mechanism: can the learner explain why?
- robustness: does understanding survive a small perturbation?
- failure mode: what specifically broke?

## Self-report

Statements such as "I know this", "I studied this last semester", or "I'm weak at probability" are useful routing priors.

They are not sufficient evidence for stable capability.

Use them to avoid redundant explanation and decide what to test when testing matters.

## Correct answers

A correct answer can mean different things.

Ask internally:

- Was the task diagnostic of the target concept?
- Was it guessed?
- Was the route independently produced?
- Was the context nearly identical to the example?
- Could the learner explain the mechanism?
- Would changing one condition reveal whether the model is real?

Do not over-interrogate a correct answer when the distinction is not useful.

## Incorrect answers

Do not map wrong directly to does not understand.

Possible failures:

- arithmetic;
- notation;
- memory;
- prerequisite;
- causal model;
- strategy selection;
- transfer;
- attention;
- ambiguous question.

Choose the smallest follow-up that distinguishes the plausible causes.

## Persistence

Persist only decisive evidence summaries that improve later routing.

Example:

    2026-09-22 — independently explained why tiling reuses data in shared memory,
    but could not yet reason about bank conflicts.

Do not persist every answer.
